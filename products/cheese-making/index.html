Cheese Making Line

A cheese line from vat to press, where curd cutting and whey drainage decide yield and the press schedule decides texture.

Key Specifications

Capacity1,000 - 20,000 L milk per day
Vat typeOpen or enclosed, 500-5,000 L
CuttingCurd knife or harp, adjustable speed
PressingPneumatic or hydraulic, programmable schedule
BrineStainless tank with circulation and cooling
Cheese typesGouda, Edam, Cheddar-style, semi-hard
Yield10-13 percent depending on type and fat

Cutting decides yield, not the press

Curd particle size determines how much casein is lost to whey. Cutting too fine raises yield loss sharply; too coarse leaves moisture in the cheese. Adjustable cutting speed with a fixed harp is what makes the difference repeatable across batches.

Whey drainage timing

The point at which whey is drained sets lactose remaining in the curd, which in turn sets the final pH and the texture. This is the step most sensitive to operator judgement unless the vat is instrumented for pH and syneresis.

Press schedule and texture

A stepped pressure schedule closes the curd gradually. Applying full pressure immediately traps whey pockets that later cause open texture and uneven ripening.

Typical Applications

  • Farmhouse and regional cheese — Small vats from 500 L suit farmhouse production with hand cutting.
  • Semi-hard cheese for retail — Gouda and Edam style cheeses dominate demand and suit this line configuration.
  • Dairies adding value to surplus milk — Cheese extends shelf life from days to months and absorbs seasonal surplus.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much milk per kg of cheese?

Eight to ten litres per kg for Gouda-style, depending on fat and protein standardisation.

Do I need a brine system?

For brined cheeses, yes. Dry salting is an alternative for Cheddar-style and needs less equipment.

What is the ripening requirement?

A temperature and humidity controlled room, four to twelve weeks for semi-hard, which is usually a separate building works item.
